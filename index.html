              Business Organisation · Group project

              ClearEntry business case

              Visa rules for studying in the EU are split across 27 national systems and change often. ClearEntry turns them into one personal, dated and sourced checklist based on passport, destination and purpose.

              In one paragraph

              About 549,400 non-EU citizens received a first EU residence permit for education in 2024 (Eurostat, 2025b), and Schengen consulates received over 12 million short-stay visa applications in 2025, refusing 14.6% (European Commission, 2026d). Rules are fragmented and moving: we logged 13 changes affecting our launch countries in 16 months. We launch with a free checker for 18 passports and all 27 EU countries (8 in depth). We earn money from universities, which lose admitted students to visa problems, and from an optional paid document check. We never sell visa appointments or pay-for-ranking.

              Section 1 · Sessions 1–2

              Problem and users

              Our first workable problem, revised with stronger sources. Changes from our first draft are marked "Revised".

              ProblemUncertainty about visa requirements costs students and travellers time and money. Unclear or outdated information leads to delayed or refused applications.
              GoalRemove the uncertainty that causes avoidable refusals and missed intakes.
              Root causesLong-stay (student) visas are national, so there are 27 procedures with no shared format. Information is spread across ministries, consulates, visa centres and universities, and rules change by decree at short notice. Revised: France applied a new funds threshold five weeks after publishing it (InfoMigrants, 2026).
              Who is affectedPrimary: non-EU students aged 18–30 applying for a degree in the EU for the first time. Secondary: travellers and families on short stays. Paying customer: universities' international offices.
              Size and evidence1.76 million students from abroad were in EU tertiary education in 2023 (Eurostat, 2025a); 549,400 first residence permits were issued for education in 2024 (Eurostat, 2025b). Short-stay refusals cost applicants €145 million in non-refundable fees in 2024, €60 million of it paid by Africans (CNN, 2025). Revised: in 2025, refusal rates approached 48% for Nigerians and passed 51% for Senegalese applicants, against a 14.6% average (European Commission, 2026d).
              Segment detailAge 18+; nationalities with weaker passports; mostly applicants from developing economies, for whom a refusal is expensive relative to income.
              Current alternativesEmbassy and consulate websites, national study agencies (Campus France, DAAD), education agents, travel-visa platforms, general AI chatbots.
              Direct competitorsExpert visa firms; AI-powered visa platforms such as Atlys (short-stay focus).

              Critical note on our own evidence

              • The "up to 50% rejection" figure in our first draft describes short-stay visas for some African nationalities. No EU-wide statistic exists for student visa refusals, so we no longer present it as a student figure.
              • Two of our original sources (GradPilot, ImmigCanada) are commercial blogs. We replaced them with European Commission and Eurostat data and kept commercial sources only where a second source agrees.
              • Not every refusal is an information problem. Refusal rates track applicants' income levels (CNN, 2025), so ClearEntry can prevent avoidable refusals (missing documents, wrong procedure, outdated amounts), not structural ones. We say so to users.

              So what?

              Four of six factors score as opportunities. The two that do not (Legal, and Economic for the student's wallet) are the same forces that make accurate guidance valuable.               Section 3 · Session 3 · Industry analysis

              Five Forces: digital visa and study-abroad guidance

              Industry defined as services that help non-EU individuals understand and complete EU entry and residence procedures (Porter, 2008).

              Threat of new entrants High

              Low capital needs; AI makes content cheap to produce. Barriers are trust, verified data and university relationships, which take years to build.

              Supplier power High

              Governments "supply" the rules and can change them or launch their own tools. Visa-centre operators (VFS, TLS, BLS) control appointments.

              Rivalry Medium

              Many players, but most focus on short-stay tourist visas or one country. Few cover long-stay study across the EU in a neutral way.

              Buyer power High

              Students: price-sensitive, with free alternatives. Universities: fewer, larger buyers, but with a real compliance need (they sponsor or vouch for students).

              Threat of substitutes High

              Official websites, international offices, agents (free to students, paid by universities), ChatGPT-style assistants, social media.

              Implication

              Generic visa information is an unattractive business: substitutes are free and entry is easy. Profit sits where we can create switching costs (university integrations) and a defensible capability (a verification routine), which shapes sections 5 and 8. Governments also act as complementors: better official portals make deep links from our checklist more useful.

              Section 6 · Session 4 · Users to value proposition

              Value proposition

              Value Proposition Canvas (Osterwalder et al., 2014) for our primary segment, first-time non-EU degree students.

              Customer profile

              Jobs: choose a destination; get admitted; get the visa in time; prove funds; arrive legally; register and renew; work part-time; stay after graduating.

              Pains: conflicting and outdated information; fear of refusal; hidden costs; losing fees and an intake; scam agents; scarce appointments; bureaucratic language.

              Gains: certainty; one list; knowing the total cost early; being able to compare countries; reassurance for parents who pay.

              Value map

              Products: personal checklist; cost panel; comparison view; change log; official links.

              Pain relievers: a date and a source on every rule; a "verify" badge where sources disagree; scam warnings; steps in the right order with timing.

              Gain creators: compare by funds, work rights and post-study stay; progress tracking; a checklist you can copy and send to a sponsor.

              Section 12 · Sessions 9–10 · Structure and human capital

              Organisation and people

              Structure

              Launch: simple functional structure. CEO (partnerships and sales), Head of Rulebook (verification and legal partners), Product and Tech, Growth (content and ambassadors).

              At scale: matrix. Destination owners (one per country, accountable for accuracy) crossed with origin-market leads (one per passport market, accountable for users). This matches the product's two dimensions: passport × destination.

              Verification workflow

              1. Monitor: official gazettes and ministry pages, weekly; alerts from university partners and users.
              2. Draft: the destination owner updates the rule with source and date.
              3. Check: a second person verifies against the official source (maker-checker).
              4. Publish: the change goes live with an entry in the public change log.

              Human capital

              • Hire multilingual verifiers with lived visa experience from our origin markets.
              • Pay student ambassadors; no unpaid internships.
              • Measure accuracy, not volume: error rate and days-to-update as key indicators.
              • Blame-free error reporting, so mistakes surface fast.

              Managing the change from project to company

              Lewin's unfreeze–change–refreeze: unfreeze by agreeing with pilot users what "accurate enough" means; change by formalising the maker-checker routine; refreeze by writing it into roles, onboarding and indicators before hiring beyond five people.

              Section 13 · Session 8 · Innovation

              Innovation

              • Type: service and business-model innovation. The rules are public; the novelty is structuring them by passport × destination × purpose, dating every rule, and having universities pay rather than students (Whittington et al., 2023).
              • Degree: incremental for users (a better checklist) but architectural for the market (it joins pieces no one else joins).
              • Open innovation: users and universities report changes; each report enters the maker-checker workflow before it goes live.
              • Deliberately not now: a free-text AI chatbot. Hallucinated visa rules would destroy trust; any assistant must answer only from the verified rulebook and disclose that it is AI.

              Section 16 · Critical thinking

              Limits of our analysis

              • PESTEL is a snapshot. Factors overlap (France's funds rule is political, economic and legal at once) and our 0–10 scores are group judgements, not measurements.
              • Five Forces assumes a clear industry. Ours mixes public services and private firms, and governments act as supplier, rival and complementor at once. The model does not capture that.
              • Survey data has biases. The Navitas survey reports agents' views; QS surveys use self-selected respondents. Neither is a random sample of students.
              • Refusal statistics cover short-stay visas only. There is no EU-wide dataset on student visa refusals, so we cannot measure the student problem directly yet.
              • Rule verification. We checked every figure on 24 September 2026, but some official sites were not directly accessible from our research tools, so several figures rely on two or more secondary sources. The checker marks these instead of hiding the uncertainty.
              • Financials are untested assumptions. No university has yet agreed to pay; the sensitivity analysis shows how much depends on this.

              Method

              How we built the rulebook

              1. Scope. 18 passports (India, China, the US, Canada, the six Gulf states, and eight other large or high-need markets) × 27 EU destinations. Eight destinations (France, Germany, the Netherlands, Spain, Italy, Ireland, Poland, Sweden) have full guides; the other 19 have basic guides. Schengen short-stay rules apply to all.
              2. Source hierarchy. Official legal text or authority page first; then the university or national agency; then reputable secondary sources, used only where two agree.
              3. Confidence labels. Every figure in the checker carries one: Official source 2+ sources Verify. "Verify" means we found one source only, or sources disagree (for example, Spain's post-study permit is given as 12 months by some sources and 24 by others).
              4. Date. All rules were checked on 24 Sep 2026. Each dated change goes into the change log.
              5. Known gap. Some official sites could not be opened directly from our research tools, so we confirmed those figures through search results and secondary sources that cite the official rule. Before publishing to real users, each "Verify" item needs a manual check on the official site.
