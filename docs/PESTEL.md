# PESTEL: our first workable problem (completed and revised, September 2026)

**Problem:** uncertainty about visa requirements costs students and travellers time and money. Unclear or outdated information leads to delayed or refused applications.

Same structure as our group template: *Key facts & 24-month outlook · Revised/additional facts · Impact on the problem · Revised impacts · Business implication · Revised business implication · Threat/Opportunity · Revised T/O*.
**Environmental** and **Legal** are new rows. Every "Revised" field was added after re-checking facts on 24 September 2026.
T/O scale: 0 = high threat, 10 = high opportunity (dot positions from our original sliders).

## Political

**Key facts & 24-month outlook**
- Studying in the EU for more than 90 days needs a national long-stay visa from the country of study, plus a residence permit after arrival. There is no single EU student visa.
- The EU Visa Strategy (Jan 2026) plans a Visa Code revision and may amend the EU student and researcher rules.
- The EU online visa platform is expected around 2028; member states can opt out for up to 7 years.
- Outlook: more EU digitalisation, but national rules stay fragmented.

**Revised / additional facts**
- Confirmed: the Commission adopted its first Visa Strategy on 29 January 2026, with a Recommendation asking states for simpler, digital long-stay procedures and a 30-day target (European Commission, 2026a). Recommendations are not binding.
- National governments moved the other way: France raised the student funds threshold by 43% (InfoMigrants, 2026), Sweden capped student work at 15 hours a week (Study in Sweden, 2026), and Poland added B2 language proof and stricter university checks (Notes from Poland, 2025).
- Visa-free travel for Qatar and Kuwait was proposed in 2022 and is still pending (European Commission, 2022).

**Impact on the problem**
- No single source: each country has its own portal, documents and sequence.
- New EU systems (EES, ETIAS) blur what applies to a student.

**Revised impacts**
- EU harmonisation and national tightening pull in opposite directions, so the number of differences a student must track is growing.
- We logged 13 rule changes affecting our 8 launch countries between May 2025 and August 2026.

**Business implication**
- Demand for one personalised tool that maps nationality, destination and programme to exact steps.
- It needs a country rulebook kept current, with "last verified" dates.

**Revised business implication**
- Monthly monitoring of national gazettes and ministry pages, with a public change log.
- Position as the layer that joins up 27 national systems, not as a rival to any government portal.

**T/O:** Opportunity, 7 → **7**

## Economic

**Key facts & 24-month outlook**
- Europe's international enrolments are projected to grow about 5% a year to 2030, faster than the US, UK, Canada or Australia.
- Euro-area growth is forecast at only 0.9% in 2026, with inflation around 3% after a new energy shock.
- France raises non-EU tuition for 2026/27 to €2,895 (bachelor) and €3,941 (master), from about €178 and €254 that most universities charged before.
- Outlook: more applicants, but each faces higher costs.

**Revised / additional facts**
- Confirmed: 5% a year for Europe vs 3.5% for the UK and between 2% and −1% for the US, Canada and Australia (QS, 2025).
- US visa delays are projected to cut foreign enrolment by more than 100,000 students this autumn (ICEF Monitor, 2026c).
- Funds thresholds jumped within 12 months: France from €615 to €877.50 a month (InfoMigrants, 2026); Italy from €6,947.33 to €10,179.85 a year, +46.5% (ConnectSaqib, 2026).
- French tuition confirmed; institutional exemptions are capped at 30% of affected students (MESR, 2026).
- **Still unsourced:** the 0.9% growth and 3% inflation figures. Cite the European Commission or ECB forecast, or remove them.

**Impact on the problem**
- Money requirements (funds proof, fees, tuition) vary and are rising, so mistakes cost more.
- A refusal means paying fees again on reapplication.

**Revised impacts**
- A student going to France or Italy must now show roughly €3,000 more per year than a year ago. An outdated figure leads straight to a refusal.
- Rejected short-stay applicants paid €145 million in non-refundable fees in 2024 (CNN, 2025).

**Business implication**
- Value proposition: prevent costly mistakes by showing the full cost and funds proof per country upfront.
- Demand is growing as students pivot to Europe.

**Revised business implication**
- "Cash to line up before you apply" is a headline feature of the checker.
- Keep the core free (students are cost-sensitive) and charge universities, which lose tuition when admitted students miss the start.

**T/O:** Mixed, 4 → **6** (mixed, leaning opportunity)

## Social

**Key facts & 24-month outlook**
- Visa uncertainty and affordability are students' top concerns in 2026. Students are anxious and weigh a wider range of destinations, including Europe.
- Students gather information from university websites, agents, friends and family, and increasingly from social media and AI tools, but trust university websites more than AI.
- Surveys in major Western European countries show majorities saying immigration has been too high, which colours the political climate around student migration.
- Outlook: the anxiety persists and more first-time applicants head to Europe.

**Revised / additional facts**
- Confirmed by the Navitas survey of 870 education agents in 64 countries (May 2026); 68% say students are more likely to give up plans because of cost (ICEF Monitor, 2026a). This measures agents' views, not students' directly.
- IDP research links visa uncertainty to a lower perceived return on study abroad (ICEF Monitor, 2026b).
- "Trust university websites more than AI" and "majorities say immigration is too high" still need a cited survey that covers international applicants. Until then we treat both as hypotheses to test in interviews.

**Impact on the problem**
- Anxious first-timers rely on peers, agents, social media and AI, which may be outdated or wrong.
- Reliance on agents and scams makes trustworthy guidance scarce and mistakes, such as fake documents, more likely.

**Revised impacts**
- Anxiety is highest where information is least reliable (informal channels).
- Parents and sponsors also need to understand the funds rules, since they often provide the money.

**Business implication**
- Serve anxious first-timers with one clear, personalised, plain-language checklist.
- It competes for attention with free social and AI channels.

**Revised business implication**
- Plain language, a legibility-first typeface, "last checked" dates and official links as trust signals.
- A checklist that can be copied and sent to sponsors; Hindi, Mandarin and Arabic versions on the roadmap.
- Distribute through universities, the source students already trust.

**T/O:** Opportunity, 8 → **8**

## Technological

**Key facts & 24-month outlook**
- EU countries are digitalising visa services unevenly. Germany's Consular Services Portal covers national visas, including study visas, at all 167 of its visa sections. France routes most residence permit applications through its ANEF portal.
- At EU level, ETIAS has slipped and the online Schengen visa platform is expected around 2028.
- Students already use AI tools heavily for search, and most AI users rate the guidance as good as traditional sources.
- Outlook: more automation on both the government and applicant side.

**Revised / additional facts**
- The Entry/Exit System (EES) has been fully operational since 10 April 2026 (European Commission, 2026b).
- ETIAS now has no launch date: the late-2026 target was removed in July 2026, and 2027 is widely expected (Fragomen, 2026).
- More national procedures moved online: Poland's MOS residence system, Sweden's fully online permits, Dutch university-led IND filing.
- EU AI Act Article 50: from 2 August 2026, chatbots must tell users they are talking to AI (European Commission, 2026c).

**Impact on the problem**
- Each country's portal, sequence and steps differ, so students must learn a new digital system per destination.
- New failure points appear: outdated AI answers and bot-resold appointments.

**Revised impacts**
- Our 8 launch countries alone use at least 8 different application portals.
- The ETIAS delay opens a scam window: fake "ETIAS" sites charge for a system that does not exist yet.

**Business implication**
- Cheap, scalable tech makes personalised checklists feasible.
- Deep-link to official portals so users can act on the guidance.

**Revised business implication**
- Use a structured rules engine, not a free-text chatbot, so every answer traces to a dated source.
- Any future AI assistant must answer only from the verified rulebook and disclose that it is AI.

**T/O:** Opportunity, 9 → **8**

## Environmental (new)

**Key facts & 24-month outlook**
- About 4 in 10 international students actively research a university's sustainability efforts (QS International Student Survey, as reported by ICEF Monitor, 2023).
- Students and educators want to reduce the carbon footprint of international education, which is dominated by flights, but report barriers (ICEF Monitor, 2025).
- Most visa procedures still need an in-person biometrics appointment, often a long journey in large countries such as India, Nigeria or China.
- Digital services use energy too: data-centre electricity use is projected to roughly double by 2030, partly because of AI (IEA, 2025).
- Outlook: more online procedures (EU platform around 2028) cut paper and travel; universities remain under pressure to report on sustainability.

**Revised / additional facts**
- Evidence gap: we found no reliable data on the emissions caused by repeat consular trips or by travel cancelled after refusals.

**Impact on the problem**
- An indirect driver. An incomplete or refused application means repeat trips to visa centres, rebooked flights and wasted travel, which costs money and carbon.

**Revised impacts**
- Low direct impact on the core problem; the main link is avoidable repeat journeys.

**Business implication**
- "Get it right at the first appointment" avoids repeat journeys.
- A lightweight static website with no always-on AI keeps our own footprint small.
- Gives universities a sustainability angle for their reporting.

**Revised business implication**
- Measure before we claim: track avoided repeat appointments through user surveys, so the benefit is evidenced rather than greenwashing.

**T/O:** Mixed, 5 → **6**

## Legal (new)

**Key facts & 24-month outlook**
- Directive (EU) 2016/801 gives students common rights: work at least 15 hours a week, stay at least 9 months after graduating to look for work, and move within the EU. States still set funds, fees and procedures; Denmark and Ireland are not bound.
- The Visa Code (Regulation (EC) No 810/2009) sets the €90 short-stay fee, the 15-day decision time and fee waivers for students on study trips; Regulation (EU) 2018/1806 decides who needs a short-stay visa.
- The GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679) covers passport, nationality and travel data.
- AI Act: transparency duties from 2 August 2026; AI used by authorities in migration and border control is classed as high-risk.
- Outlook: a Visa Code revision and possible amendments to the students directive (EU Visa Strategy); ETIAS legal duties once it launches.

**Revised / additional facts**
- National rules change by decree at short notice: France's Decree 2026-526 was published on 24 June 2026 and applied from 1 August 2026, five weeks later (Kohen, 2026).
- We have not yet had a lawyer check whether giving individual immigration advice is a regulated activity in each launch country, so the product gives general information only.

**Impact on the problem**
- The legal complexity is the problem itself.
- Changes at short notice catch careful applicants out between preparing and submitting.

**Revised impacts**
- Liability risk: if our information is wrong and causes a refusal, a user could claim damages.

**Business implication**
- "Information, not legal advice" terms; a source and a date on every rule.
- Privacy by design: the checker runs in the browser and stores nothing on a server.

**Revised business implication**
- Maker-checker verification, professional indemnity insurance, and lawyer partners for premium reviews.
- Legal review of our terms in each launch country before charging users.

**T/O:** Mixed, high threat, 4 → **4** (mixed, leaning threat)
